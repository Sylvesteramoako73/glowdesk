To calculate stylist commission, add up the revenue each stylist generated from the services they performed in the pay period, then multiply it by their agreed commission percentage. For example, a stylist who brought in GHS 8,000 of services in a month on a 30% commission earns GHS 2,400. The maths is simple. The hard part is agreeing what counts as revenue, recording every service against the right person, and showing your team the figures so they trust them.

## The main ways salons pay stylists

Salons in Ghana use a few common pay structures. Many use a mix depending on the stylist's experience.

### Straight commission

The stylist earns a percentage of the service revenue they generate and nothing else. It rewards busy, skilled stylists and keeps your costs in line with your income, but it can be tough for new stylists who are still building a client base.

### Salary plus commission

The stylist earns a fixed monthly amount plus a smaller commission on their services, or a commission only above a target. This gives your team stability and still rewards performance. It suits salons that want stylists to also help with tasks that do not bring in direct revenue, such as washing, cleaning and assisting.

### Chair or booth rental

The stylist pays the salon a fixed rent for a chair and keeps the money from their own clients. You have predictable income, but less control over prices, hours and the client experience, and the stylist effectively runs their own business inside yours.

### Apprentices

Apprentices are usually learning rather than earning commission, and many salons pay a monthly stipend instead. As an apprentice's skills develop, some salons move them onto a small commission for the services they can perform on their own.

## A worked example

Say a stylist on 30% commission performed these services in a month:

| Service | Price (GHS) | Number | Revenue (GHS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knotless braids | 450 | 8 | 3,600 |
| Wash and set | 150 | 14 | 2,100 |
| Silk press | 250 | 6 | 1,500 |
| Treatment add on | 100 | 8 | 800 |
| **Total** | | | **8,000** |

Commission is 30% of GHS 8,000, which is **GHS 2,400**.

If the same stylist were on a salary of GHS 1,000 plus 15% commission, they would earn GHS 1,000 plus GHS 1,200, which is **GHS 2,200**.

## Decide what counts as revenue

Most disagreements about commission come from unclear rules. Write down, and share with your team, answers to these questions:

- **Is commission based on the price charged or the price paid?** If you give a discount, does the stylist's commission fall too? Most salons calculate on what the client actually paid.
- **What about products?** Many salons pay a separate, smaller commission on retail products the stylist sells, or none at all.
- **Who pays for the hair and supplies?** For services that use expensive hair or products, some salons deduct the cost before calculating commission. Agree this upfront.
- **What happens with a redo?** If a client returns because something went wrong, decide whether the redo is free and whether commission is affected.
- **Tips** usually belong to the stylist and are not part of commission.

## Record every service against the right person

Commission is only as accurate as your records. If services are written in a notebook or remembered at month end, mistakes are inevitable, and stylists will notice.

Record each service when it is paid for, with the stylist's name, the service and the amount. When two people work on one client, such as a stylist and an assistant on a long braiding job, decide in advance how that service is split.

## Be transparent with your team

Stylists trust commission they can see. At the end of each pay period, give each stylist a simple statement showing:

- The services they performed and the revenue from each
- Their commission rate
- Any agreed deductions
- The total commission due

When the numbers come from the same system used at checkout, there is far less room for argument, and stylists can raise any missing service before payday rather than after.

## Keep an eye on the bigger picture

Commission is usually your largest cost, so watch it alongside your other expenses. If commission, rent, utilities and supplies together take up almost all your revenue, the salon has little left to grow. Track your expenses each month and look at net profit, not just takings.

It also helps to compare stylists fairly. Revenue per stylist, number of clients and the share of clients who return show you who is growing the business, and who may need support or training.
